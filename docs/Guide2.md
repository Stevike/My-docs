# Technical Troubleshooting and Maintenance Documentation Guide

## 1. Introduction

This guide demonstrates how technical troubleshooting information can be organized to help engineers, technicians, and maintenance personnel identify equipment problems, investigate possible causes, document findings, and determine appropriate next steps.

It uses a general industrial equipment example to demonstrate technical writing practices. It is not a substitute for equipment-specific troubleshooting instructions or an approved maintenance procedure.

## 2. Purpose

The purpose of this guide is to demonstrate a consistent approach to technical troubleshooting by helping readers:

- Identify and describe equipment symptoms.
- Gather relevant operating and maintenance information.
- Investigate possible causes systematically.
- Record inspection findings and corrective actions.
- Verify the outcome and maintain accurate maintenance records.

## 3. Scope

This guide provides a general framework for troubleshooting industrial equipment and instrumentation.

Actual troubleshooting activities must follow the applicable manufacturer's documentation, site procedures, risk assessment, and authorization requirements.

## 4. Safety Precautions

Before investigating an equipment problem:

- Review the applicable risk assessment and work instructions.
- Obtain the required work authorization.
- Identify electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, and process-energy hazards.
- Apply approved isolation and lockout/tagout procedures where required.
- Verify the equipment is in a safe condition before inspection.
- Use appropriate personal protective equipment.
- Ensure only competent and authorized personnel perform technical tests and repairs.

**Warning:** Never loosen pressurized fittings, open process connections, bypass safety devices, or work on energized equipment unless an approved procedure specifically permits the activity and the required safeguards are in place.

## 5. Troubleshooting Workflow

### Step 1: Describe the Problem

Record the reported symptom as clearly and objectively as possible.

Examples include:

- Equipment fails to start.
- An instrument reading appears inconsistent.
- A system generates an alarm.
- Equipment performance differs from the expected operating condition.
- A component shows visible damage or leakage.

Record the date, equipment identification, operating conditions, alarm codes, and circumstances under which the problem occurred, where available.

### Step 2: Review Available Information

Review relevant technical information before deciding on corrective action.

This may include:

- Equipment manuals and technical data sheets
- Electrical or instrumentation drawings
- Previous maintenance reports
- Alarm and event logs
- Inspection records
- Calibration certificates
- Approved operating procedures

Confirm that the documentation applies to the actual equipment model and configuration.

### Step 3: Identify Possible Causes

Develop a list of potential causes based on the observed symptoms and available evidence.

| Observed Symptom | Potential Cause | Information to Review |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment does not start | Power supply, control signal, interlock, or mechanical issue | Electrical drawings, alarm history, and approved start-up procedure |
| Instrument reading is unstable | Sensor issue, loose connection, interference, or changing process conditions | Instrument specification, signal records, and calibration history |
| Unexpected alarm | Operating condition outside configured limits or instrument fault | Alarm log, configured limits, and equipment documentation |
| Reduced equipment performance | Operating conditions, wear, blockage, or control issue | Performance history, inspection records, and manufacturer guidance |
| Visible leakage | Seal, fitting, connection, or component damage | Equipment drawings, inspection history, and approved isolation procedure |

these causes are not confirmed diagnoses. The actual cause must be established through appropriate inspection and testing.

### Step 4: Investigate and Record Findings

Follow the approved troubleshooting procedure for the specific equipment.

Record:

- The checks performed
- The instruments and tools used
- Relevant readings and observations
- Any alarm codes or error messages
- Deviations from the expected condition
- The evidence supporting or ruling out each suspected cause

Do not make assumptions where measurements or inspection evidence are unavailable.

### Step 5: Determine the Corrective Action

Select corrective action based on verified findings, equipment requirements, and the applicable maintenance procedure.

Depending on the findings, the next step may involve further diagnosis, adjustment by authorized personnel, component replacement, calibration, repair, or escalation to the manufacturer or responsible engineering team.

Record the approved action and the person or team responsible for carrying it out.

### Step 6: Verify the Outcome

After authorized corrective work is completed:

1. Confirm that required inspections and checks have been completed.
2. Verify that the equipment is reassembled and restored in accordance with the applicable procedure.
3. Perform the prescribed functional or operational checks.
4. Compare the results with the approved acceptance criteria.
5. Record the outcome and any outstanding issues.
6. Return the equipment to service only after the required authorization has been obtained.

If the problem remains unresolved, document the outstanding symptoms and escalate the issue through the approved technical support process.

## 6. Troubleshooting Report Template

Use the following template to demonstrate consistent technical recordkeeping.

| Field | Information to Record |
|---|---|
| Report reference | Unique report or work-order number |
| Equipment identification | Equipment name, tag number, model, and serial number, where applicable |
| Date and time | When the issue was reported and investigated |
| Reported symptom | Description of the observed problem |
| Operating conditions | Relevant conditions at the time of the issue |
| Initial assessment | Preliminary observations and potential causes |
| Investigation performed | Checks, measurements, and inspections completed |
| Findings | Evidence collected during the investigation |
| Root cause | Confirmed cause, if established |
| Corrective action | Approved repair or action taken |
| Verification results | Results against the applicable acceptance criteria |
| Final status | Resolved, unresolved, or awaiting further investigation |
| Prepared and reviewed by | Names, roles, and dates |

## 7. Example Troubleshooting Record

The following is an illustrative example showing how a troubleshooting report can present findings without claiming an unverified diagnosis.

**Reported issue:** An industrial pressure indicator displays a fluctuating reading.

**Initial assessment:** The cause has not been confirmed.

**Investigation plan:**
- Review the instrument specification and installation documentation.
- Compare the displayed reading with available operating records.
- Check for relevant alarms and recent maintenance activity.
- Arrange inspection and testing by an authorized technician in accordance with the approved procedure.

**Finding:** Pending inspection and verification.

**Corrective action:** To be determined from the investigation results.

**Verification:** The instrument must be assessed against the applicable manufacturer requirements and approved acceptance criteria before the issue can be closed.

This example demonstrates the difference between a reported symptom, a suspected cause, a verified finding, and an approved corrective action.

## 8. Common Documentation Mistakes

Avoid the following practices when preparing troubleshooting documentation:

- Presenting a suspected cause as a confirmed diagnosis.
- Recording measurements without units or relevant operating context.
- Recommending repairs without checking equipment-specific requirements.
- Omitting equipment identification or document references.
- Failing to record inspection results and corrective actions.
- Closing a report without documenting verification results.
- Using outdated drawings, manuals, or procedures.

Clear records improve traceability and help other personnel understand what was investigated and what remains unresolved.

## 9. Summary

A structured troubleshooting process supports clear communication, evidence-based diagnosis, consistent maintenance records, and appropriate escalation of unresolved issues.

Effective technical documentation distinguishes symptoms from causes, records the evidence collected, identifies approved corrective actions, and documents verification before an issue is closed.
